[Market vocabulary](../../foundations/market-vocabulary/) deals with markets
calling the same thing by different names. This page deals with the harder case: markets that
are **shaped differently**, in ways that invalidate assumptions most systems bake in on day one.

Three assumptions in particular:

1. *A policy has one insurer.*
2. *A product is one thing, approved once.*
3. *A policy is either in force or not.*

Each is false in at least one major market.

## The London subscription market

A large risk placed at Lloyd's is not written by an insurer. It is written by **several
syndicates, each taking a percentage line**, recorded on a slip. The broker builds the
placement until the risk is fully subscribed.

<Ledger>
  <LedgerRow term="Slip">
    The placing document. Records the risk, the terms, and which syndicates have taken what
    share.
  </LedgerRow>
  <LedgerRow term="Written line vs signed line">
    Underwriters write for more than they intend to keep when a risk is oversubscribed; lines are
    then scaled down proportionally. The **signed line** is the final binding share.
    <Eg>a syndicate writes 15% on a risk subscribed to 150%; its signed line is 10%.</Eg>
  </LedgerRow>
  <LedgerRow term="Leader / following market">
    The lead underwriter negotiates terms and typically drives claims decisions; followers take
    the same terms on their share.
  </LedgerRow>
</Ledger>

<Example label="Worked example">
A ₹500 crore energy risk is placed across four syndicates with signed lines of 40%, 30%, 20%
and 10%. Annual premium is ₹5 crore.

Premium splits **40 / 30 / 20 / 10** → ₹2 cr, ₹1.5 cr, ₹1 cr, ₹0.5 cr.

A ₹80 crore loss splits on the same shares → ₹32 cr, ₹24 cr, ₹16 cr, ₹8 cr.

The policy is one contract with **four** premium receivables and four claim payables, and each
participation is separately reported, reserved and settled. *(Illustrative figures.)*
</Example>

<SystemsNote>
"Insurer" cannot be a foreign key on the policy. A policy carries an **ordered set of
participations**, each with a party, a signed share, and its own financial postings.

Systems built single-carrier retrofit this as a "co-insurance" side table, and then discover
that premium, commission, claims, reserves, reinsurance and bordereaux all need to split by
share — which means the share belongs in the core model, not bolted beside it.
</SystemsNote>

## The US: admitted, surplus lines, and fifty regulators

Insurance in the US is regulated **by state**. There is no single national product approval.

<Ledger>
  <LedgerRow term="Admitted (licensed)">
    The insurer is licensed in that state and its rates and forms are filed with the state
    regulator. Policyholders are protected by the state guaranty fund.
  </LedgerRow>
  <LedgerRow term="Surplus lines (non-admitted)">
    For risks the admitted market declines. The carrier is not licensed in that state, is
    generally free from rate and form filing, and the policy attracts a **surplus lines tax**.
    No guaranty fund backing.
    <Eg>a coastal property or an unusual cyber exposure that admitted carriers won't quote.</Eg>
  </LedgerRow>
  <LedgerRow term="Rate and form filing">
    Admitted products must have their wordings and rates approved — state by state, with
    different rules and different effective dates in each.
  </LedgerRow>
</Ledger>

<SystemsNote>
**A "product" in the US is a matrix, not a record.** One product across 50 states is up to 50
approved variants, each with its own forms, rate tables and effective dates — and they do not
change in step.

This is the single largest driver of product-configuration complexity in US platforms, and it
is why `systems/product-configuration` is a release-management problem rather than a data-entry
one. It also means **admitted status is a policy attribute with tax and compliance
consequences**, not a property of the carrier alone.
</SystemsNote>

## Compulsory covers differ, so validation differs

What the law forces someone to buy varies by market, and mandatory cover is where hard
validation rules live.

<Ledger>
  <LedgerRow term="India">
    Motor third-party liability is compulsory under the Motor Vehicles Act.
  </LedgerRow>
  <LedgerRow term="United Kingdom">
    Motor third-party and **employers' liability** are compulsory for most employers.
  </LedgerRow>
  <LedgerRow term="United States">
    Workers' compensation is mandated at state level, with rules and monopolistic state funds
    varying between states.
  </LedgerRow>
</Ledger>

<SystemsNote>
Compulsory cover is the one place a system should refuse to issue. But *which* cover is
compulsory is jurisdictional, so the rule belongs in **market-scoped configuration**, never in
application code — otherwise entering a new market means a release rather than a config change.
</SystemsNote>
