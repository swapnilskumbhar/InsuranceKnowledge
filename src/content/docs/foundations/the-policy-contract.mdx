The **policy** is the legal contract. Four parts, and your users will see them named this way
on the document, so name them this way in your system too.

<Ledger>
  <LedgerRow term="Declarations page">
    Who and what is covered, the limits, the period, and the premium. The summary page a
    policyholder actually reads.
    <Eg>a motor schedule showing the registration number, IDV, period of insurance and premium.</Eg>
  </LedgerRow>
  <LedgerRow term="Insuring agreement">
    The promise itself — what the insurer will do when a covered event occurs.
  </LedgerRow>
  <LedgerRow term="Exclusions">
    What is not covered. The section that generates the most claim disputes, and therefore the
    section your claim decisioning logic spends the most time in.
    <Eg>hydrostatic lock is excluded from standard motor own-damage cover — it needs an add-on.</Eg>
  </LedgerRow>
  <LedgerRow term="Conditions">
    Obligations on both sides — notification deadlines, duty to minimise loss, cancellation rights.
  </LedgerRow>
</Ledger>

<SystemsNote>
The document's four sections do **not** map to four tables. Declarations are the *current
state of a time-varying aggregate*; the insuring agreement and exclusions belong to the
**product version** the policy was written on, not to the policy row.

Copying wording text onto each policy record is the common shortcut. It works until the first
mid-term endorsement, at which point you cannot say which wording version was in force on the
date of loss — which is the only question a claims decision actually needs answered.
</SystemsNote>
