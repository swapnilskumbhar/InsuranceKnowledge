**Insurance is risk transfer.** A person or business that cannot absorb a large loss pays a
small, certain **premium** to an insurer who can — because the insurer pools thousands of
similar risks. The **law of large numbers** makes losses predictable across the pool even
though any single loss is not.

<Example label="Worked example">
10,000 car owners each pay a ₹10,000 premium → a ₹10 crore pool. In a typical year around
300 of them claim, averaging ₹2.5 lakh each → ₹7.5 crore in losses. The remaining ₹2.5 crore
covers commissions, operations, reinsurance and profit.

No single owner could absorb a ₹2.5 lakh loss comfortably; the pool absorbs 300 of them
predictably. *(Illustrative figures.)*
</Example>

## Premium is a forecast, not a price

The number above — 300 claims at ₹2.5 lakh — is an *estimate made before the year happens*.
Everything downstream inherits that fact. A price is something you look up. A forecast is
something you version, revise, and later reconcile against what actually occurred.

This is the difference that most system designs get wrong on the first attempt.

<SystemsNote>
Because premium is a forecast, **rating tables are versioned data, not configuration**. You
need to answer "what rate did we charge this policy, on this date, under which filed
version" years after the fact — for regulators, for disputes, and for the actuaries
re-estimating next year's rates from last year's experience.

A rate table stored as a current-state lookup destroys exactly the history that makes the
next forecast possible.
</SystemsNote>
